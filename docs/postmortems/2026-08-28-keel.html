Why it merged

The intervention thesis failed

Keel was built to steer attention — shields that blocked sites, budgets that rationed time, signals that nudged. Every one was retired by mid-2026 after research review showed static interventions increase workload for high-perceived-control users.

What survived was the observation layer: the log, the watchlist, the fences that read accumulated signal rather than enforcing static rules. Keel stopped being a product and became infrastructure.

Zenborg needed what keel observed

The kairos-direction pivot recognized that zenborg's garden — "where will I place my consciousness today?" — demands the raw attention signal keel produces. The tool demands input it should derive. The log is that derivation source. Two repos for one pipeline was indirection.

The tray's uptime data sealed it: dark for 1096 of 1342 hours tracked, with 670 hours of machine-in-use time lost across 55 gaps. The writer needed a process that stays up, and zenborg already was that process.

What we learned

Observation is the product; intervention is a trap.

Every intervention keel shipped was retired within four months. The log outlived all of them. Building for "steering" led to walls the user resented; building for "seeing" led to data the garden can metabolize.

Uptime is an architecture problem, not a daemon problem.

Three launchd plists, a menubar tray, and a browser extension were four separate answers to "how does this stay running." The real answer: be part of the app the user already runs.

Separate repos for coupled systems create drift.

Keel and zenborg shared a vault, a substrate contract, and a user. Two repos meant two vault implementations and a seam that went stale twelve minutes after it was written.

Retire early, retire visibly.

Every keel retirement was stamped — a decision artifact with rationale, not a quiet deletion. That discipline made this postmortem possible and each retirement reversible in principle.
